# The AI Upskilling Program — Course Catalog

**A four-level program that meets you where you actually are.**

*Version 1.1 · Reviewed against the live catalog on 2026-07-17 · 8 classes · 32 lessons*

---

## How to read this catalog

This program has **four levels**. Each level is defined by **what you actually do on a Monday
morning** — not by a test score, and not by how much you've read about AI.

Three things worth knowing before you scan the list:

**You are not expected to reach Level 4.** Most people shouldn't, and don't need to. Level 4 is the
architect track — a small number of people design the systems everyone else uses. For most roles,
**Level 2 is the goal**, and that is a genuine, complete, respectable destination. This catalog says
so out loud because most programs quietly imply the opposite.

**Every level is a real place to live, not a waiting room.** You are not "behind" at Level 1. Level 1
is where most of the workforce is, and the program is built to make you effective *there* before it
asks you to go anywhere else.

**Classes never skip a level.** A class bridges you from where you are to the next step — never two
steps. If a class assumes something you haven't met yet, that's our bug, not your gap.

**Every lesson ends with a thing you made.** 15–30 minutes, and you finish holding an artifact you
can use at work — a prompt library, a context file, a one-pager. There are no quizzes anywhere in
this program.

## Level 1 — AI-Aware

> *"I've heard of it. I've maybe tried it. I'm not sure it's for my job."*

**What this looks like on a Monday:** you open an AI tool now and then to ask a question or summarize
something, usually because someone suggested it. Your prompts are short. You eyeball the output and
think "seems right." You often forget AI could have helped at all.

**What Level 1 gets you:** targeting AI at specific low-risk tasks — drafting an email, summarizing a
doc, outlining notes — and following one safety rule every single time. That's it. That's the level,
and it's worth having.

## Level 2 — AI-Enabled

> *"AI is a normal tool in my week. I know when to use it and when not to."*

**This is the target for most roles.** If you get here and stop, the program worked.

**What this looks like on a Monday:** you use AI several times a week on real work — summarizing a
PRD, drafting tickets, outlining a test plan. Your prompts have context, a task, and an audience. You
reuse patterns from a team library instead of reinventing. You always edit the output before it goes
anywhere. You can explain, in plain words, when AI is the wrong choice.

## Level 3 — AI-Fluent

> *"I design how my team uses AI, and I maintain what we share."*

**This is the coach-track target.** You're shaping how AI gets used, even if you're not building
systems.

**What this looks like on a Monday:** you've defined a repeatable workflow someone else runs. You
curate the team's prompt library and teach people when to use it. You think in risk tiers — AI stays
on reversible, read-only work, and anything touching customers, regulators, or production gets a
human review.

## How you get placed

**We place you by watching how you work, not by asking you to rate yourself.** Self-assessment
over-places nearly everyone — including the people who wrote this.

Five observable checks:

1. **Unprompted use** — did you reach for AI this week without being told to? *(No → Level 1)*
2. **Prompt shape** — do your prompts include context, task, and audience? *(No → Level 1)*
3. **Reuse** — do you use a shared prompt library? *(Use → Level 2. Maintain it → Level 3)*
4. **Workflow design** — have you defined a repeatable AI workflow someone else runs? *(Yes → Level 3)*
5. **System design** — have you built schemas, evaluations, or governance others work inside?
   *(Yes → Level 4)*

There is no penalty for placing at Level 1. It is the most common result.

## Catalog health — an honest look at coverage

Included so reviewers can see what's thin, not just what exists.

| Level | Population | Classes | Distinct lessons | Per learner | Plain-language lessons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Aware** | **Most of the workforce** | 2 | **11** | **9 (~195 min)** | **7** |
| 2 — Enabled | The broad middle | 3 | 9 | 4 (~100 min) | 0 |
| 3 — Fluent | A minority | 1 | 5 | 5 (~145 min) | 0 |
| 4 — Native | A small percentage | 2 | 7 | 3–4 (~75–120 min) | 0 |

**What changed on 2026-07-17, and why.** Learners told us there wasn't enough Level 1 content — and
crucially, *why*: **they don't believe they'll move past it.** That wasn't impatience. It was a correct
reading of our own catalog. Level 1 held most of the workforce on five lessons and under two hours,
while Level 3 — explicitly "a minority" — had *more* per-learner minutes. Population was a steep
pyramid; the catalog was flat.

Worse, only **three** Level 1 lessons were plain-language; the rest already reached for patterns. So
the rung most people would actually live on was the thinnest rung in the program, and it read as a
**waiting room** — five lessons that end by handing you off to Level 2.

**So we didn't add more on-ramps to Level 2. We added depth at the plain rung**, and Level 1 became a
destination: **9 lessons, ~195 minutes, 7 of them plain language.** The two lessons doing the heaviest
lifting (A5 Red Lines, A6 Did It Get It Right?) existed before only as a single sub-section of a
15-minute hook lesson — the two things a nervous beginner needs most, sharing one paragraph.

**The remaining honest gap:** Level 2 still has no plain-language lesson at all. If the same feedback
arrives from Level 2 cohorts, this is where it will come from.

Level 1 depth shipped 2026-07-17. Next: a **submission and feedback loop** so learners can hand in an
