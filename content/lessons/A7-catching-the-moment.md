# Catching the Moment: Where AI Fits in Your Week

> You had the first win. It worked. You liked it. Then you didn't open the tool again for three weeks. That's not fear and it's not distrust — those get their own lessons. It's that the moment never announced itself. This is the most common way Level 1 quietly ends, and it's fixable in twenty-five minutes with your calendar.

## Start Here: Last Week's Repeats (5 minutes)

Open last week's calendar and your sent folder.

Find three things you did **more than once**. Not three things you'd *like* to hand off. Three things you actually did twice.

The list is usually boring. The status update. The same explanation to two different people. Reformatting someone else's notes. Boring is the point — boring and repeated is exactly where your twenty minutes are hiding. Nobody's minutes are hiding in the interesting work.

## Why You Forget (7 minutes)

Here's what's actually happening, and it's worth saying plainly because most people read it as a character flaw:

**The moment doesn't announce itself.**

When the Friday update lands on you, nothing says "this is one of those." It just feels like the Friday update — the same as it felt for the last four years. You're not under-informed. You've had the training. You could pass a quiz on this. **You're un-cued.**

That's the whole mechanism. Knowing that AI could help is a fact you already have. *Noticing, at 4:40pm on a Friday, that this specific chore is one of the ones* — that's a different thing entirely, and no amount of additional knowledge produces it.

Which is why "I'll try to use it more" never works. You're asking your memory to fire at a moment your memory has no reason to notice. It won't. It never has, for anything.

## Attach It to Something That Already Happens (7 minutes)

So don't rely on remembering. **Attach it to a thing that already interrupts you.**

Not: *"I'll use AI more for updates."*
But: *"When the Friday update lands in my calendar, I open the tool first."*

The difference matters more than it looks. The first one needs you to remember. The second one needs your calendar to remember, and your calendar is better at this than you are and never has a bad week.

Good cues are things that already happen without you: a recurring meeting, a standing deadline, a specific email that always arrives, the moment you sit down after standup. Bad cues are feelings — "when I have time," "when I'm stuck." Those never arrive on schedule.

Pick the cue first. Then the moment attaches to it.

## Assignment

Five moments. One flagged. Then wait a week — the waiting is part of it.

### Deliverables

- my-moments.md — five recurring moments. For each: the moment, the trigger ("when I'm about to…"), and where it recurs (which meeting, which ritual, which email)
- Check each one against your red lines and cut any that cross one. If that leaves you with three, that's a true five-item list with two honest holes — say so
- Flag one as **this week's**
- Next week, come back and mark which triggers actually fired. **That mark is the lesson** — the list is just the setup
