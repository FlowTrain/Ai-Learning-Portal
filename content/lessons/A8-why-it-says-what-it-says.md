# Why It Says What It Says

> Ask ten people what this thing actually is and you'll get two answers: magic, or glorified autocomplete. Neither one helps you decide whether to use it on Tuesday. There's one sentence that explains it, and almost everything else in this program falls out of that sentence — including why it's sometimes wonderful and why it's sometimes confidently, fluently wrong.

## Start Here: Finish the Sentence (3 minutes)

Write down how you'd finish this, right now, before you read on:

> "AI is basically a ______."

Don't overthink it. Whatever you'd say to your neighbor. Keep it somewhere — you'll write it again at the end, and the gap between the two is the entire lesson.

## The One Thing It Does (7 minutes)

Here it is:

> **It writes what plausibly comes next, based on what's in front of it.**

That's the whole thing. It has read an enormous amount of writing, and it has become extraordinarily good at one narrow trick: given some text, produce what would most plausibly follow.

That's not a put-down. "What would plausibly follow" is a *staggeringly* useful thing to be good at, because most of what we write at work is, honestly, fairly predictable — and the predictable parts are the parts that eat your afternoon.

But notice what's *not* in that sentence. It doesn't say "it looks up the answer." Unless you've handed it something to read, or someone has deliberately connected it to a source, **it isn't looking anything up.** It's producing what would plausibly follow. Sometimes what would plausibly follow is the correct answer — often, even. That's not the same as having checked.

Mine is: it's like the most well-read colleague you've ever met, answering instantly, from memory, at a party, with total confidence, and no way to look anything up. That analogy is fine. Yours will be better, because yours will come from your own work.

## Everything Else Follows From That (7 minutes)

Three things fall out of that one sentence. You've met all three already; this is why they're true.

**Why more context changes the answer.** If it produces what plausibly comes next *based on what's in front of it*, then changing what's in front of it changes what comes next. "Write an update" and "write an update, for my skeptical director, about this project, here are the notes" aren't the same request with more decoration — they're genuinely different situations, so they get genuinely different plausible continuations. That's why you tell it three things. Not politeness. Mechanics.

**Why it can be smooth and wrong at the same time.** It's aiming at *what usually follows*. What usually follows is usually true — that's why it works at all. But "usually" isn't "always," and here's the sting: **nothing in the writing changes when it slips from usually-true to not-true.** The tone doesn't wobble. Fluency isn't evidence of anything except fluency. That's the whole reason you need a check that doesn't depend on how the text feels.

**Why what you hand it matters.** What you give it is what it works from. That's the useful version — and it's the same reason your red lines exist. It isn't a vault; it's a workspace. What you put on the desk is what's on the desk.

One sentence. Three consequences. That's your picture of how it works, and it will hold up better than any list of tips, because you can extend it to situations nobody taught you.
