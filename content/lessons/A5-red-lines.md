# The Red Lines: What Never Goes In

> The fastest way to stop using AI isn't a bad answer. It's a colleague saying "careful what you put in that" — and you, having no idea where the line is, quietly drawing it around everything. This lesson gets you a short list, in your own words, of what never goes in and what you do instead. One card. Then the fear stops making your decisions for you.

## Start Here: The Elevator Test (5 minutes)

Open the last thing you pasted into an AI tool. If you can't think of one, open the last thing you *almost* pasted and backed out of — that hesitation is the more interesting artifact anyway.

Read it, and mark anything you wouldn't read aloud in a crowded elevator.

That's it. That's the instinct we're going to make explicit. You already have it; you just haven't written it down, which is why it fires as vague dread instead of a clear decision.

## The Three Questions That Draw Your Lines (7 minutes)

Most people try to memorize a list of forbidden things and give up, because the list is infinite. Ask three questions instead. Any "yes" is a red line.

**Is it someone else's to share?** A customer's name. A colleague's performance conversation. A partner's numbers. If the person it belongs to didn't hand it to you for this, it isn't yours to paste.

**Would a leak be impossible to take back?** Some mistakes you can fix with an apology. Others you can't. Unreleased plans, anything under an agreement you signed, anything that would be a headline. The test isn't how likely it is to go wrong — it's whether you could undo it if it did.

**Would I be embarrassed to be quoted?** Not "is it technically allowed" — would you be comfortable if this exact text turned up in a screenshot with your name on it? Your gut is faster than any rulebook and it's usually right.

Three questions, and they generate the list for you. That's better than a list, because next month there'll be a new kind of document nobody wrote a rule for, and you'll still know the answer.

## What to Do Instead (5 minutes)

Here's where most people go wrong: they turn "be careful" into "never use it," and then they're back to doing everything by hand. **A red line isn't a stop sign. It's a swap.**

**Strip the names.** Most of the time it's the *identifiers* that are sensitive, not the problem. "Acme is 60 days late on invoice 4471" becomes "a client is 60 days late on an invoice." The AI can help with the second one just as well, and it never needed the first.

**Describe the shape instead of pasting the thing.** You don't have to paste the contract to ask how people usually structure a termination clause. Describe what you're dealing with; ask about the kind, not the case.

**Ask about the category, not your instance.** "How do teams usually handle X?" gets you most of the value of "here's our X, fix it," with none of the exposure.

**And sometimes: do it by hand.** That's a legitimate answer, not a failure. Some tasks aren't worth the care they'd require. Knowing which is which *is* the skill.

## Assignment

Write your card. Keep it short enough that you'd actually remember it standing at your desk deciding whether to paste something.

### Deliverables

- my-red-lines.md — three to five red lines, each one line, each with an "instead, I…"
- One line at the bottom: where your organization's actual rules live (a link is fine — find it now, so you're not looking for it in the moment)
- One sentence: the line you'd have crossed last month if nobody had said anything
