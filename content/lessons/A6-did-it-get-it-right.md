# Did It Get It Right?

> Two people take this lesson. One used an AI answer, thought "seems right," sent it, and has carried a low hum of unease ever since. The other won't touch the tool because they can't tell if it's making things up. Same missing skill, opposite symptoms, both stuck. Neither of them is going to learn statistics today. You'll leave with three questions you can actually run in a minute.

## Start Here: How Do You Know? (5 minutes)

Take the last useful thing an AI gave you. Don't reopen the source.

Now write one sentence: **how do you know it was right?**

Most people write some version of "it sounded right." If that's your honest answer, you're exactly who this lesson is for. That's not a personal failing — it's the normal answer, and it's the whole problem in one sentence. Fluent writing feels like correct writing. Our brains have used that shortcut our entire lives, and it has worked, because until recently anything that well-written had a person behind it who knew things.

## Confident and Wrong Is the Normal Failure (5 minutes)

Here's the part people get backwards. They expect AI to be wrong the way a broken machine is wrong — obviously, loudly, with something clearly off.

It isn't. **It's wrong the way a very poised person who didn't do the reading is wrong.** Smooth, plausible, well-organized, and untrue. Nothing in the tone changes when it stops knowing. There's no wobble, no hedge, no tell.

That's not a bug someone forgot to fix. It's the normal, expected behavior of a thing that produces what *usually* comes next — and what usually comes next is usually true, but "usually" isn't "always." (If you've done *Why It Says What It Says*, this is that same one sentence, coming back to collect.)

Which means: **you cannot catch it by reading more carefully.** Careful reading detects awkwardness, and there won't be any. You need a check that doesn't depend on how the text feels.

## Your Three Questions (5 minutes)

Short enough to run in under a minute. Write them in your own words — these are mine.

**Where would this have come from?** If it's telling you a fact, a number, a name, a date — is there a place that fact could plausibly live? "It knows because it's read a lot" is not a source. If you can't name where it *would* have learned this, treat it as a guess wearing a suit.

**Does it match one thing I already know for certain?** Just one. You almost always know *something* about the topic — pick the fact you're surest of and check that. If it got the easy one wrong, stop reading and start over. If it got the easy one right, you've learned a little, not a lot.

**What would it look like if it were wrong — and could I see that from here?** This is the one that does the work. A wrong summary looks like a summary. A wrong number looks like a number. If the answer is "wrong would look exactly like this," then **you cannot check this from where you're standing**, and that's your signal to go look at the source or ask someone who knows.

## Assignment

Write the check, then run it once on something real today.

### Deliverables

- my-check.md — three or four questions in your own words, short enough to run in a minute
- A "caught it" log at the bottom, with the first entry filled in: one real time AI got something wrong, and what tipped you off. (If you genuinely don't have one yet, go find one — run your check on three things today and something will turn up. That hunt *is* the assignment.)
- One line: a kind of mistake your check would miss, and who you'd ask about it
