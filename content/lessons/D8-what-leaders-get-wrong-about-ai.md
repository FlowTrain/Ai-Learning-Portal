# What Leaders Get Wrong About AI

> You funded the rollout. Seats are deployed, the dashboard says adoption is climbing, and yet six months in the only people finishing real work with it are the engineers who'd have figured it out on their own. That's not a model problem, and it's usually not a people problem. It's four quiet beliefs that send an AI budget to the wrong place. This is literacy, not architecture — you're learning to fund it, measure it, and place it well, so your architects build the right thing.

## Start Here: Name the Payoff You Assumed (5 minutes)

Take one AI investment you've made or are weighing — a license rollout, a pilot, a platform. Write, in one line, the payoff you assumed it would produce. Hold that line; we'll test it against four things that are easy to get backwards.

## Usage Doesn't Teach It (5 minutes)

The most expensive assumption is that the tool learns your organization the more you use it. It doesn't. The model's knowledge is fixed; nothing about it changes between sessions from your usage. What improves is the *context you supply* — your standards, your templates, your examples — and that improvement lives in those documents, not in usage volume.

The frame that keeps the money in the right place: you're not training a colleague who gets to know you over time. You're writing very good onboarding docs for an extremely fast contractor with no memory. **The docs are the asset.** Fund the context and the standards, not the hope that more clicks accrue institutional knowledge.

## Seats Aren't Adoption (5 minutes)

Seats deployed behind a tool that no one outside engineering voluntarily opens is not adoption — it's shelfware with a login. The only measure that means anything is *completed work product by people who aren't engineers*.

So the question for your next review isn't how many licenses are active. It's: **how many people outside engineering finished something real with it last month?** If that number is small, more seats won't fix it, and neither will more training — the gap is usually that the tool never met them in the work they already do.

## One Tool Isn't the Front Door for Everything (5 minutes)

The last one is a category error. There are two different jobs, and one tool rarely does both well. A **reception desk** answers role-based questions and triggers workflows, lands where people already are, and is governed centrally — excellent, and not the same thing as a **workshop** that builds real artifacts through an iterate-and-verify loop. Someone always asks the reception desk to produce a spec, and is always disappointed. Decide which one you're funding before the pilot, not after.

And when someone hands you a recommendation by brand — "we'll use Copilot" — ask *which* one. Products that share a name can behave completely differently, and the specifics change fast; that's a question for your architects, not an assumption to fund on.

## Assignment

Pressure-test one real AI investment against the four.

### Deliverables

- `ai-investment-read.md` — for one AI spend:
  - **where the value actually accrues** (context/standards/documents vs. "the tool will learn us")
  - **the adoption measure that means something** (work finished by non-engineers, named specifically)
  - **a front-door-vs-workshop call** — which job is this, and what's the other kind of tool for
- One line: something you'll **stop** funding because this read exposed it as the wrong bet
