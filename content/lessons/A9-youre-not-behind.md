# You're Not Behind

> You watched someone make the tool do something impressive, and a quiet voice said: everyone else gets this, and you don't. That voice has one thing backwards. A tool being hard to *read* is not the same as you being bad at it — and most of what feels like "I can't do this" is really the tool failing to tell you what it's doing. This lesson is about telling those two apart, so the next confusing moment doesn't send you all the way back to not using it at all.

## Start Here: The Moment It Made You Feel Dumb (5 minutes)

Think of one time an AI tool made you feel behind. It sat there doing nothing, or it did something strange, or you finished and still weren't sure it had worked.

Write one sentence about what happened. Don't fix it, don't explain it — just name it. We'll come back to it.

## It's Legibility, Not You (5 minutes)

Here is what was usually *actually* wrong in that moment: you couldn't tell whether the thing was working, waiting on you, or broken. No spinner, no "done," no undo, no way to see where it was. That is not a gap in your knowledge. It's the tool not showing its state.

There's a plain word for this: **legibility** — can you read what the thing is doing. Most of what people call "too technical" is really "I couldn't tell what was happening." And that reframes the whole feeling, because the useful question stops being *what's wrong with me* and becomes: **what did I want to know and couldn't?** That question you can actually answer — and it almost always points at the tool, not at you.

## You Don't Need Their Workflow (5 minutes)

The other trap is believing you have to learn the *developer* way of working — the black screen, the typed commands, the setup steps — before you're allowed to get value. You don't.

Start where you already are, with the things you already make: a document, an email, a spreadsheet, a summary, a first draft. The tools sometimes look like they belong to engineers, and honestly, some of them do — but that's a front-door problem for the people who build the tools to solve, not a competence problem for you. You are not late to a race. You're being handed a tool through an awkward doorway.

## A Passing Check Isn't Proof (5 minutes)

One last one, because it's the flip side of feeling behind — sometimes the tool makes you feel *too* good. The answer comes back clean, well-organized, confident, so it must be right.

Not necessarily. A polished answer can be smoothly, completely wrong, and nothing in how it looks will warn you. (You'll get three quick ways to catch that in *Did It Get It Right?*.) For now, hold just one idea: **a clean-looking answer is a draft to check, not a verdict.** Feeling behind and over-trusting a nice-looking answer turn out to be the same mistake — reading the surface instead of the state.

## Assignment

Turn the moment you named at the top into something you can act on.

### Deliverables

- `not-behind.md` — the moment you wrote down, rewritten as a **state question** the tool failed to answer ("was it working? did it finish? did it need me?")
- One thing **you already make** that you'll try AI on this week — not a developer task, your actual work
- One line: an answer that *looked* right, which you'll double-check before you trust it — and how
